## When to use

Use this when you want to strengthen your support system so you have people to turn to when urges are high or when you feel isolated. It helps when you notice you are trying to do recovery alone or when you have lost touch with people who support your goals.

## Steps

1. **List 2–3 people** you can contact when you are struggling. They do not have to be in recovery themselves—they need to be people who respect your goals and will not encourage you to use. Write their names and how you can reach them (call, text, etc.).

2. **Reach out when you are not in crisis.** Send a check-in or say you are working on recovery and would like to stay in touch. Building the habit when you are calm makes it easier to reach out when you are not.

3. **Consider peer support.** Groups, meetings, or online communities can provide people who “get it” and who have been through similar urges and setbacks. You do not have to share a lot—listening can be enough at first.

4. **Tell at least one person your plan.** “If I have a strong urge, I’m going to call you before I do anything.” That creates accountability and gives you a concrete step when the urge hits.

5. **Review your list regularly.** People move, numbers change, relationships shift. Every few weeks, check that your list is still accurate and that you have actually contacted these people recently.

## Notes

- Support is not a sign of weakness. Many people in recovery say that connection was a key part of staying on track.
- If you have burned bridges, you can still build new connections—groups, a counselor, or new friends who support your goals.
- This is supportive coaching only, not a substitute for professional addiction treatment or mental health care.
