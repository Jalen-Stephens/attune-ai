## When to use

Use this when you have a night when sleep is not coming easily and you want to reduce frustration and still support your body. It is for times when you have already tried your usual wind-down and you are still awake.

## Steps

1. **Shift the goal from “fall asleep” to “rest.”** Lying still in a dark, quiet room with your eyes closed still gives your body some recovery. Reducing the pressure to sleep can lower anxiety that keeps you awake.

2. **Use a simple relaxation focus.** Breathe at a comfortable pace; notice the weight of your body on the mattress. If your mind wanders, gently bring attention back without criticizing yourself.

3. **Avoid clock-watching.** Turn the clock away or cover it. Checking the time often increases worry about how little sleep you are getting and can make it harder to relax.

4. **Stay in bed if you are calm.** If you are not highly frustrated, you can keep resting. If you are tense or angry about being awake, get up and do something calm until you feel drowsy again.

5. **Remind yourself that one night does not define you.** Poor sleep happens. How you respond to it—with rest and self-compassion rather than panic—can make the next night easier.

## Notes

- Rest is not a substitute for addressing ongoing sleep problems, but it can make difficult nights more manageable.
- If you often lie awake for long periods, consider keeping a simple log (bedtime, wake time, how you feel) to spot patterns and discuss with a professional if needed.
- This is supportive guidance only, not medical or sleep-disorder treatment.
