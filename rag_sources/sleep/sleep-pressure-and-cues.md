## When to use

Use this when you want to understand why you sometimes feel wide awake at bedtime or tired at the wrong times. It is useful when you are trying to align your schedule with natural sleep drive and environmental cues.

## Steps

1. **Sleep drive (pressure)** builds the longer you are awake. If you nap late or sleep in a lot, you may have less drive at night. Reducing long or late naps can help nighttime sleep feel more natural.

2. **Light is a strong cue.** Bright light in the morning and daytime supports alertness and a stable rhythm. In the evening, dimming lights and reducing blue light can signal that sleep time is near.

3. **Consistency matters.** Waking and going to bed at roughly the same time most days—including weekends—helps your body expect sleep at a certain hour.

4. **Use the bed mainly for sleep.** If you lie in bed for a long time awake, your brain can learn to associate the bed with being alert. Getting up and doing something calm until you feel sleepy, then returning to bed, can help.

5. **Track what helps.** Notice which days you sleep better: same wake time? Less caffeine after noon? A shorter wind-down? Small adjustments often add up.

## Notes

- You do not need to achieve a perfect schedule. Small, steady changes usually work better than big overnight shifts.
- If you have a night of poor sleep, try to still get up at your usual time the next day to keep your rhythm from shifting.
- This is general coaching only; it is not a replacement for evaluation of medical sleep issues.
