## When to use

Use this when you notice you rely on feeling motivated to get things done—and when motivation is low, nothing happens. It helps when you want to build structure that works even on low-energy or low-focus days.

## Steps

1. **Identify one recurring task** that often gets dropped when you do not feel like it: paying bills, exercise, meal prep, checking email, or a work deliverable.

2. **Attach it to a trigger.** Link the task to something that already happens: “After I pour my morning coffee, I open my calendar” or “When I get home, I put my keys in the bowl and then I put my bag in the same place.” The trigger is the cue; the task follows.

3. **Make the first step tiny.** If the task is “exercise,” the first step might be “put on workout clothes” or “stand on the mat.” If the task is “pay bills,” the first step might be “open the bills folder.” You can stop after the first step if you want—often doing one step leads to the next.

4. **Reduce friction.** Put what you need in the way: clothes laid out, document open, folder on the desktop. Remove what gets in the way: phone in another room, notifications off. Make the system do the work so you do not have to decide each time.

5. **Review weekly.** Did the trigger happen? Did you do the step? If not, was the step too big, the trigger wrong, or was something else in the way? Adjust and try again.

## Notes

- Systems do not replace motivation; they reduce how much you need it. On bad days, the system can still carry you partway.
- One system at a time is enough. Add more once one feels steady.
- This is practical coaching only, not a substitute for evaluation or treatment of ADHD or executive function challenges.
